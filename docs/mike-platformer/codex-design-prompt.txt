You are designing a browser 2D platform game. Read docs/mike-platformer/brief.md first; it is the owner's brief and its constraints are absolute, above all: no real machine-tool brands, logos, model names, colour schemes or recognisable features anywhere, because the game shows rogue broken machines and must never imply a real manufacturer makes bad machines. Then look at docs/mike-platformer/reference/ (the signed-off Mike puppet SVG with its joint documentation, a poses preview, the brand lock-up and colours) and skim public/fireworks/little-patterns/studio-lab.html for the house conventions of games on this host (fixed logical resolution, pointer handling, iPad 5 as the slow target, headless-Chrome harnesses).

Write docs/mike-platformer/level-1-design.md: a complete, buildable design for Level 1 with the framework for more levels. Write it for a developer (Opus 5.5) who will build the whole game from it without asking questions, and for the owner, who is not a developer and judges by feel. Plain English, precise numbers where a developer needs them, no filler.

Cover, in this order:

1. One-paragraph pitch and the tone: Sonic pace with Mario readability; a stylised, cheerful, slightly menacing rogue machine shop; family-friendly; Mike never fights, he fixes.
2. The world and level framework: what a "level" is (a machine to reach and fix), how levels chain, what carries over, what a level template contains, so Levels 2 to N can be authored from data not code. Name three or four future machines/levels as examples with one line each, all fictional and generic.
3. Mike: moves and controls. Run, jump (height, variable jump, coyote time, jump buffering, in numbers relative to a tile size you define), crouch/slide if wanted, ledge behaviour, conveyor interaction, what happens on hit (knockback and invulnerability window), lives and the SD card as extra life, checkpoints. Touch layout for iPad (left/right, jump, camera) and keyboard mapping. How the jointed puppet animates for each state (run cycle, jump, land, hit, camera pose, fix animation) using its named joints, and what a beat of "gesticulation" costs nothing.
4. The video-camera time mechanic, designed properly: what it does (slow, pause, rewind, or record-and-replay, choose one and justify), how it is charged and spent, its readable feedback (screen effect, sound, a meter), what it can and cannot affect, at least three concrete obstacles in Level 1 that need it and how each is solved, and how it stays fun rather than a crutch. Include the failure case and the cool-down.
5. Hazards and enemies for Level 1, each with a name, a one-line description, size in tiles, behaviour with timings, how it hurts, how it is avoided, and its tell (the readable warning). Include: crashed-part spitters (parts fly out on a rhythm), grabbing multi-axis spindle arms (reach, sweep pattern, the safe zone), conveyors (speed, direction changes, gaps between belts), pits, and two more of your invention that fit a machine shop (coolant, swarf, chips, chuck jaws, tool changers are fair game). All machines are fictional and generic; describe their look without any brand cue.
6. Collectibles: spare parts (types, how many in the level, where they sit: on the path, off the path as reward, guarded), lubricants (what they do: a small heal or a temporary speed?), control units (what they do), SD cards (extra life, how rare, where hidden), and the score/tally the player sees.
7. Level 1 layout: a section-by-section walkthrough from start to boss, about 8 to 12 sections, each with its purpose (teach a mechanic, test it, breather, set piece), its hazards, its collectibles, its length in tiles, and a rough ASCII map. Target play time 4 to 6 minutes for a first run. Difficulty curve stated explicitly. Where the checkpoints are.
8. The boss: the repair sequence. Mike has arrived at the broken machine with the parts he collected. Design it as a puzzle of dodging, not fighting: what the spindle(s) do (patterns, phases, tells), where Mike must go to fit each part, how many parts, what fitting a part looks like and how long it takes (a held action that can be interrupted), what happens if he arrives with parts missing (design the fair answer), how the camera mechanic figures in the fight, the phases as the machine comes back to life, and the win moment. Keep it readable on a phone.
9. Presentation: art direction for the machine shop in a vector/flat cartoon style consistent with the puppet (palette built from the brand colours plus machine-shop greys and safety yellow, no real-world brand cues), parallax layers, the HUD (parts tally, lives as SD cards, camera meter), fonts (Helvetica Neue is licensed), and where the MTDCNC lock-up appears (title, level card, results; never on a machine).
10. Sound: a short list of sounds and a music direction, all synthesised or licence-clean, with the owner's known preference for subtle, non-harsh audio.
11. Technical notes for the builder: fixed logical resolution and scaling, tile size, physics step, collision approach, data-driven level format (show the JSON shape for a level), performance budget for the iPad 5, touch and keyboard input, save of progress in localStorage only, no network, no accounts, test harness expectations.
12. Scope for more levels: what is level-specific data versus engine, and an honest list of what Level 1 leaves unfinished.
13. Open questions for the owner, each with your recommended answer.

Constraints on your writing: British English. No emoji. Do not name any real machine-tool manufacturer or product, even as a "not like". Keep it under about 4,500 words. Write the file and nothing else.
